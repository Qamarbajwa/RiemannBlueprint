0.5 Reciprocal Euler Product, Möbius Inversion, and the Mertens Equivalence

In Section 0.4, we established that the unweighted composite zeta function \(\zeta _C(s)\) and the divisor-weighted series \(Z_{\mathrm{weighted}}(s) = (\zeta (s) - 1)^2\) capture the additive and multiplicative projections of the composite integers \(\mathbb {C}_{\mathrm{comp}}\). We now turn our investigation to the reciprocal of the Riemann zeta function, \(1/\zeta (s)\), which serves as the dual multiplicative filter of the arithmetic integers. Whereas the direct zeta function \(\zeta (s) = \sum n^{-s}\) sums all integers with uniform positive unit weight, its inverse inverts the Euler product, introducing signed arithmetic weights governed by the Möbius function \(\mu (n)\).

0.5.1 Derivation of the Reciprocal Euler Product

Theorem 0.5.1 Reciprocal Euler Product & Möbius Dirichlet Series
✓

For every complex number \(s \in \mathbb {C}\) with \(\operatorname {Re}(s) {\gt} 1\), the reciprocal of the Riemann zeta function admits the absolutely convergent Euler product expansion:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:reciprocal_euler_product} \frac{1}{\zeta (s)} = \prod _{p \in \mathbb {P}} \left(1 - \frac{1}{p^s}\right). \end{equation}
55

Furthermore, the term-by-term expansion of this product across the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic yields the Dirichlet series:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:mobius_dirichlet_series} \frac{1}{\zeta (s)} = \sum _{n=1}^\infty \frac{\mu (n)}{n^s}, \end{equation}
56

where \(\mu : \mathbb {N}\to \{ -1, 0, 1\} \) is the Möbius arithmetic function defined by:

\begin{equation} \mu (n) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } n = 1, \\ (-1)^k & \text{if } n = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k \text{ with distinct primes } p_i \text{ (square-free)}, \\ 0 & \text{if } p^2 \mid n \text{ for some prime } p \text{ (square-divisible)}. \end{cases} \end{equation}
57

The series ?? converges absolutely in the half-plane \(\operatorname {Re}(s) {\gt} 1\).

Proof ▶

Let \(s = \sigma + i t\) with \(\sigma {\gt} 1\). By Euler’s product formula (Euler’s product identity ??), \(\zeta (s) = \prod _{p \in \mathbb {P}} (1 - p^{-s})^{-1}\). Since \(\sum _{p} |p^{-s}| = \sum _{p} p^{-\sigma } {\lt} \sum _{n=1}^\infty n^{-\sigma } {\lt} \infty \), the product converges absolutely. An absolutely convergent infinite product of non-zero factors is non-zero, and its reciprocal is the product of the reciprocals of its factors:

\[ \frac{1}{\zeta (s)} = \frac{1}{\prod _{p \in \mathbb {P}} (1 - p^{-s})^{-1}} = \prod _{p \in \mathbb {P}} \left(1 - p^{-s}\right). \]

Now consider the finite partial product over all primes up to \(X {\gt} 2\):

\[ \Pi _X(s) \coloneqq \prod _{p \le X} \left(1 - \frac{1}{p^s}\right). \]

Expanding this finite product by applying the distributive law over the finite set of primes \(\{ p \in \mathbb {P}: p \le X\} \):

\[ \Pi _X(s) = 1 + \sum _{k=1}^{\pi (X)} (-1)^k \sum _{p_1 {\lt} p_2 {\lt} \dots {\lt} p_k \le X} \frac{1}{(p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k)^s}. \]

By the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic (Theorem 0.2.4), every product \(n = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k\) of distinct primes represents a unique square-free positive integer whose prime factors are all \(\le X\). Conversely, every square-divisible integer \(n\) contains at least one squared prime factor \(p^2 \mid n\), which cannot arise from expanding \(\prod (1 - p^{-s})\) where each factor is of degree at most \(1\) in \(p^{-s}\). Hence:

\[ \Pi _X(s) = \sum _{\substack {n \le X \\ \mu (n) \neq 0}} \frac{\mu (n)}{n^s} + \sum _{\substack {n {\gt} X \\ p \mid n \implies p \le X \\ \mu (n) \neq 0}} \frac{\mu (n)}{n^s}. \]

Subtracting the full Dirichlet sum \(\sum _{n=1}^\infty \mu (n) n^{-s}\):

\[ \left| \Pi _X(s) - \sum _{n=1}^\infty \frac{\mu (n)}{n^s} \right| \le \sum _{n {\gt} X} \frac{|\mu (n)|}{n^\sigma } \le \sum _{n {\gt} X} \frac{1}{n^\sigma }. \]

Because \(\sigma {\gt} 1\), the tail sum \(\sum _{n {\gt} X} n^{-\sigma }\) converges to \(0\) as \(X \to \infty \):

\[ \lim _{X \to \infty } \sum _{n {\gt} X} \frac{1}{n^\sigma } = 0. \]

Therefore:

\[ \frac{1}{\zeta (s)} = \lim _{X \to \infty } \Pi _X(s) = \sum _{n=1}^\infty \frac{\mu (n)}{n^s}. \]

Finally, absolute convergence follows immediately from \(|\mu (n) n^{-s}| \le n^{-\sigma }\) and \(\sum n^{-\sigma } {\lt} \infty \) for \(\sigma {\gt} 1\).

For \(\operatorname {Re}(s) {\gt} 1\), the reciprocal zeta function decomposes into the exact tripartite identity:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:reciprocal_partition} \frac{1}{\zeta (s)} = 1 - P(s) + \zeta _{\mu , C}(s), \end{equation}

where \(P(s) = \sum _{p \in \mathbb {P}} p^{-s}\) is the Prime Zeta function, and \(\zeta _{\mu , C}(s)\) is the Möbius-signed Composite Dirichlet Series:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:mobius_composite_series} \zeta _{\mu , C}(s) \coloneqq \sum _{c \in \mathbb {C}_{\mathrm{comp}}} \frac{\mu (c)}{c^s} = \sum _{\substack {c \in \mathbb {C}_{\mathrm{comp}}\\ \mu (c) \neq 0}} \frac{(-1)^{\omega (c)}}{c^s}. \end{equation}
62

Proof ▶

By Theorem 0.5.1, \(\frac{1}{\zeta (s)} = \sum _{n=1}^\infty \mu (n) n^{-s}\). Partitioning the index set \(\mathbb {N}\) into \(\{ 1\} \), \(\mathbb {P}\), and \(\mathbb {C}_{\mathrm{comp}}\):

\[ \sum _{n=1}^\infty \frac{\mu (n)}{n^s} = \frac{\mu (1)}{1^s} + \sum _{p \in \mathbb {P}} \frac{\mu (p)}{p^s} + \sum _{c \in \mathbb {C}_{\mathrm{comp}}} \frac{\mu (c)}{c^s}. \]

We evaluate each term:

  1. For \(n = 1\): \(\mu (1) = 1\), so \(\mu (1) 1^{-s} = 1\).

  2. For \(n = p \in \mathbb {P}\): \(p\) has exactly one prime factor (\(\omega (p) = 1\)) and is square-free, so \(\mu (p) = (-1)^1 = -1\). Thus:

    \[ \sum _{p \in \mathbb {P}} \frac{\mu (p)}{p^s} = -\sum _{p \in \mathbb {P}} \frac{1}{p^s} = -P(s). \]

  3. For \(n = c \in \mathbb {C}_{\mathrm{comp}}\): If \(c \in \mathbb {C}_{\mathrm{pure}}\) or if \(c\) is divisible by any square (\(p^2 \mid c\)), then by definition \(\mu (c) = 0\). Such composites are completely annihilated. If \(c\) is square-free with prime factorization \(c = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k\) (\(k = \omega (c) \ge 2\)), then \(\mu (c) = (-1)^k\).

Summing these three contributions gives identity ??.

Remark 0.5.3 The Parity Cancellation Mechanism
#

Identity ?? exposes the algebraic mechanism of the Möbius function:

  • Square-Divisible Annihilation: Every composite with repeated prime factors (including all pure prime powers \(p^a\) with \(a \ge 2\)) carries weight \(\mu (c) = 0\). They exert zero direct influence on \(1/\zeta (s)\).

  • Even-Odd Parity Balancing: Square-free composites are partitioned into two opposing camps:

    \begin{align*} \mathbb {C}_{\mathrm{comp}}^{(+)} & = \{ c \in \mathbb {C}_{\mathrm{comp}}: \mu ^2(c) = 1, \; \omega (c) \equiv 0 \pmod2\} = \{ 6, 10, 14, 15, 21, 22, 26, 33, 34, 35, \dots \} , \\ \mathbb {C}_{\mathrm{comp}}^{(-)} & = \{ c \in \mathbb {C}_{\mathrm{comp}}: \mu ^2(c) = 1, \; \omega (c) \equiv 1 \pmod2\} = \{ 30, 42, 66, 70, 78, 102, 105, 110, 114, \dots \} . \end{align*}

    For \(c \in \mathbb {C}_{\mathrm{comp}}^{(+)}\), \(\mu (c) = +1\), which reinforces the leading unit \(+1\). For \(c \in \mathbb {C}_{\mathrm{comp}}^{(-)}\), \(\mu (c) = -1\), which reinforces the prime deflation \(-P(s)\). The cancellation between \(\mathbb {P}\cup \mathbb {C}_{\mathrm{comp}}^{(-)}\) and \(\{ 1\} \cup \mathbb {C}_{\mathrm{comp}}^{(+)}\) governs the magnitude of \(1/\zeta (s)\).

Lemma 0.5.5 Mellin Transform Representation of \(1/\zeta (s)\)
✓
#

Uses

For every \(s \in \mathbb {C}\) with \(\operatorname {Re}(s) {\gt} 1\), the reciprocal Riemann zeta function can be expressed as the Laplace–Mellin integral of the Mertens function:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:reciprocal_mellin} \frac{1}{\zeta (s)} = s \int _1^\infty \frac{M(x)}{x^{s+1}} \, \mathrm{d}x. \end{equation}
64

Proof ▶

Let \(s = \sigma + i t\) with \(\sigma {\gt} 1\). We apply Abel summation (integration by parts of the Riemann–Stieltjes integral) to the partial sums of the Dirichlet series \(\sum _{n=1}^N \mu (n) n^{-s}\):

\[ \sum _{n=1}^N \mu (n) n^{-s} = \int _{1^-}^N x^{-s} \, \mathrm{d}M(x). \]

Integrating by parts:

\[ \int _{1^-}^N x^{-s} \, \mathrm{d}M(x) = \left[ M(x) x^{-s} \right]_{1^-}^N - \int _1^N M(x) \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}(x^{-s}) \, \mathrm{d}x. \]

Since \(M(1^-) = 0\) and \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}(x^{-s}) = -s x^{-s-1}\), we obtain:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:abel_finite_mertens} \sum _{n=1}^N \frac{\mu (n)}{n^s} = \frac{M(N)}{N^s} + s \int _1^N \frac{M(x)}{x^{s+1}} \, \mathrm{d}x. \end{equation}
65

We now analyze the boundary term as \(N \to \infty \). By the trivial bound \(|\mu (n)| \le 1\):

\[ |M(N)| = \left| \sum _{n \le N} \mu (n) \right| \le \sum _{n \le N} 1 = N. \]

Therefore:

\[ \left| \frac{M(N)}{N^s} \right| = \frac{|M(N)|}{N^\sigma } \le \frac{N}{N^\sigma } = N^{1-\sigma }. \]

Because \(\sigma {\gt} 1\), \(1 - \sigma {\lt} 0\), which implies:

\[ \lim _{N \to \infty } \frac{M(N)}{N^s} = 0. \]

Furthermore, the improper integral converges absolutely:

\[ \int _1^\infty \left| \frac{M(x)}{x^{s+1}} \right| \mathrm{d}x \le \int _1^\infty \frac{x}{x^{\sigma +1}} \, \mathrm{d}x = \int _1^\infty x^{-\sigma } \, \mathrm{d}x = \left[ \frac{x^{1-\sigma }}{1-\sigma } \right]_1^\infty = \frac{1}{\sigma - 1} {\lt} \infty . \]

Taking the limit \(N \to \infty \) on both sides of ?? gives:

\[ \frac{1}{\zeta (s)} = \sum _{n=1}^\infty \frac{\mu (n)}{n^s} = s \int _1^\infty \frac{M(x)}{x^{s+1}} \, \mathrm{d}x, \]

which establishes ??.

0.5.4 The Mertens–Riemann Equivalence Theorem

The integral representation ?? forms a profound bridge between the rate of growth of the arithmetic sum \(M(x)\) and the location of the non-trivial zeros of \(\zeta (s)\).

Theorem 0.5.6 Mertens–Riemann Equivalence
✓
#

Uses

  • Lemma 0.5.5

Lean declarations

The Riemann Hypothesis is equivalent to the statement that for every \(\epsilon {\gt} 0\):

\begin{equation} \label{eq:mertens_bound} M(x) = \mathcal{O}\left(x^{\frac{1}{2} + \epsilon }\right) \quad \text{as } x \to \infty . \end{equation}
66

Specifically, \(\zeta (s)\) has no zeros in the half-plane \(\operatorname {Re}(s) {\gt} \frac{1}{2}\) if and only if ?? holds.

Proof ▶

We prove both directions of the equivalence:

Direction 1: ?? \(\implies \) Riemann Hypothesis.

Assume that for every \(\epsilon {\gt} 0\), \(M(x) = \mathcal{O}(x^{1/2 + \epsilon })\). Then for any fixed \(\epsilon {\gt} 0\), there exist constants \(C_\epsilon {\gt} 0\) and \(x_0 \ge 1\) such that for all \(x \ge 1\):

\[ |M(x)| \le C_\epsilon x^{\frac{1}{2} + \epsilon }. \]

Consider the integral in ??:

\[ I(s) \coloneqq s \int _1^\infty \frac{M(x)}{x^{s+1}} \, \mathrm{d}x. \]

We examine the domain of absolute convergence of this integral. For \(s = \sigma + i t\) with \(\sigma {\gt} \frac{1}{2} + \epsilon \):

\[ \int _1^\infty \left| \frac{M(x)}{x^{s+1}} \right| \mathrm{d}x \le C_\epsilon \int _1^\infty \frac{x^{\frac{1}{2} + \epsilon }}{x^{\sigma +1}} \, \mathrm{d}x = C_\epsilon \int _1^\infty x^{-\left(\sigma - \frac{1}{2} - \epsilon + 1\right)} \, \mathrm{d}x = \frac{C_\epsilon }{\sigma - \frac{1}{2} - \epsilon } {\lt} \infty . \]

Thus, the integral converges absolutely and uniformly on any compact subset of the half-plane:

\[ U_\epsilon \coloneqq \left\{ s \in \mathbb {C}: \operatorname {Re}(s) {\gt} \frac{1}{2} + \epsilon \right\} . \]

By standard complex analysis (differentiation under the integral sign), \(I(s)\) defines a holomorphic function on \(U_\epsilon \). Since \(\epsilon {\gt} 0\) was arbitrary, \(I(s)\) defines a holomorphic function on the entire half-plane:

\[ \mathcal{H}_{1/2} \coloneqq \left\{ s \in \mathbb {C}: \operatorname {Re}(s) {\gt} \frac{1}{2} \right\} . \]

By Lemma 0.5.5, \(I(s) = \frac{1}{\zeta (s)}\) on the half-plane \(\operatorname {Re}(s) {\gt} 1\). By the Identity Theorem for holomorphic functions, \(I(s)\) is the unique analytic continuation of \(1/\zeta (s)\) to the entire half-plane \(\mathcal{H}_{1/2}\).

Now suppose for the sake of contradiction that \(\zeta (s)\) has a zero \(\rho _0 \in \mathcal{H}_{1/2}\), so that \(\operatorname {Re}(\rho _0) {\gt} \frac{1}{2}\) and \(\zeta (\rho _0) = 0\). Since \(\zeta (s)\) is meromorphic on \(\mathbb {C}\) and not identically zero, \(\rho _0\) is an isolated zero of order \(m \ge 1\). Consequently, its reciprocal \(1/\zeta (s)\) has an isolated pole of order \(m\) at \(s = \rho _0\):

\[ \lim _{s \to \rho _0} \left| \frac{1}{\zeta (s)} \right| = \infty . \]

However, \(I(s)\) is holomorphic on all of \(\mathcal{H}_{1/2}\), meaning \(|I(s)|\) remains finite in a neighborhood of \(\rho _0\). This is a direct contradiction. Therefore, \(\zeta (s)\) cannot possess any zeros in \(\operatorname {Re}(s) {\gt} \frac{1}{2}\). By the functional equation \(\xi (s) = \xi (1-s)\) (which relates zeros at \(\rho \) to zeros at \(1 - \rho \)), any zero with \(\operatorname {Re}(\rho ) {\lt} 1/2\) would imply a zero at \(1 - \rho \) with \(\operatorname {Re}(1 - \rho ) {\gt} 1/2\). Hence, all non-trivial zeros of \(\zeta (s)\) must satisfy \(\operatorname {Re}(\rho ) = \frac{1}{2}\).

Direction 2: Riemann Hypothesis \(\implies \) ??.

Assume the Riemann Hypothesis holds: all non-trivial zeros satisfy \(\operatorname {Re}(\rho ) = \frac{1}{2}\). Then the function \(f(s) \coloneqq \frac{1}{\zeta (s)}\) is holomorphic in the open half-plane \(\operatorname {Re}(s) {\gt} \frac{1}{2}\). By Perron’s formula applied to the Dirichlet series \(\frac{1}{\zeta (s)} = \sum _{n=1}^\infty \mu (n) n^{-s}\), for \(c {\gt} 1\) and \(x \ge 2\) not an integer:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:perron_mertens} M(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \int _{c - i T}^{c + i T} \frac{1}{\zeta (s)} \frac{x^s}{s} \, \mathrm{d}s + R(x, T), \end{equation}
67

where the truncation error satisfies:

\[ R(x, T) = \mathcal{O}\left( \frac{x^c}{T (c - 1)} + \frac{x \ln x}{T} \right). \]

We construct a rectangular contour \(\Gamma \) with vertices \(c - i T\), \(c + i T\), \(\sigma _0 + i T\), and \(\sigma _0 - i T\), where:

\[ \sigma _0 \coloneqq \frac{1}{2} + \epsilon , \qquad 0 {\lt} \epsilon {\lt} \frac{1}{2}. \]

Under RH, \(f(s) = 1/\zeta (s)\) has no poles inside or on the contour \(\Gamma \). By Cauchy’s Integral Theorem:

\[ \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint _{\Gamma } \frac{1}{\zeta (s)} \frac{x^s}{s} \, \mathrm{d}s = 0. \]

Therefore:

\[ \frac{1}{2\pi i} \int _{c - i T}^{c + i T} \frac{1}{\zeta (s)} \frac{x^s}{s} \, \mathrm{d}s = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \left( \int _{\sigma _0 - i T}^{\sigma _0 + i T} + \int _{\sigma _0 + i T}^{c + i T} - \int _{\sigma _0 - i T}^{c - i T} \right) \frac{1}{\zeta (s)} \frac{x^s}{s} \, \mathrm{d}s. \]

Under the Lindelöf Hypothesis for \(1/\zeta (s)\) (which is a consequence of RH, see Titchmarsh  [ 18 ] , Theorem 14.2), for any \(\delta {\gt} 0\) and \(\sigma \ge \frac{1}{2} + \epsilon \):

\[ \left| \frac{1}{\zeta (\sigma + i t)} \right| = \mathcal{O}\left( |t|^\delta \right) \quad \text{as } |t| \to \infty . \]

We estimate the integrals along each segment of the contour:

  1. Along the vertical segment \([\sigma _0 - i T, \sigma _0 + i T]\):

    \[ \left| \int _{\sigma _0 - i T}^{\sigma _0 + i T} \frac{1}{\zeta (s)} \frac{x^s}{s} \, \mathrm{d}s \right| \le x^{\sigma _0} \int _{-T}^T \frac{|\zeta (\sigma _0 + i t)|^{-1}}{\sqrt{\sigma _0^2 + t^2}} \, \mathrm{d}t \le x^{\frac{1}{2} + \epsilon } \int _{-T}^T \mathcal{O}\left( (1 + |t|)^{\delta - 1} \right) \mathrm{d}t = \mathcal{O}\left( x^{\frac{1}{2} + \epsilon } T^\delta \right). \]

  2. Along the horizontal segments \([\sigma _0 \pm i T, c \pm i T]\):

    \[ \left| \int _{\sigma _0 \pm i T}^{c \pm i T} \frac{1}{\zeta (s)} \frac{x^s}{s} \, \mathrm{d}s \right| \le \int _{\sigma _0}^c \frac{|\zeta (\sigma \pm i T)|^{-1}}{T} x^\sigma \, \mathrm{d}\sigma = \mathcal{O}\left( \frac{T^\delta x^c}{T \ln x} \right). \]

Choosing \(c = 1 + \frac{1}{\ln x}\), \(T = x^2\), and \(\delta {\lt} \frac{\epsilon }{4}\), all error terms are dominated by \(x^{1/2 + \epsilon }\). Hence:

\[ M(x) = \mathcal{O}\left(x^{\frac{1}{2} + \epsilon }\right). \]

This completes the bidirectional proof.

Remark 0.5.7 Historical Context on Mertens’ Conjecture
#

In 1897, Franz Mertens conjectured the stronger bound \(|M(x)| {\lt} \sqrt{x}\) for all \(x {\gt} 1\)  [ 13 ] . This conjecture would have implied the Riemann Hypothesis and the simplicity of all zeros. However, in 1985, Odlyzko and te Riele  [ 16 ] disproved Mertens’ conjecture by showing:

\[ \limsup _{x \to \infty } \frac{M(x)}{\sqrt{x}} {\gt} 1.06 \quad \text{and} \quad \liminf _{x \to \infty } \frac{M(x)}{\sqrt{x}} {\lt} -1.009. \]

Subsequent refinements by Kotnik and van de Lune (2004) showed the extrema exceed \(1.218\) and \(-1.229\). Crucially, the failure of the absolute constant bound \(|M(x)| \le \sqrt{x}\) in no way invalidates the asymptotic exponent bound \(M(x) = \mathcal{O}(x^{1/2 + \epsilon })\), which remains an exact, unconditional equivalence of the Riemann Hypothesis.
